Guide

Cumulant generating functions

A CGF wraps a Julia function K(t) of a vector t of length $d$ (also for $d = 1$, where you write t[1]), together with the dimension and, optionally, a predicate describing the domain:

K = CGF(t -> -3*log(1 - t[1]) - 2*log(1 - 2t[2]), 2; domain = t -> t[1] < 1 && t[2] < 1/2)

The function is evaluated on vectors of truncated multivariate Taylor series to generate its derivatives, and on BigFloat values for variable precision. It must therefore be written generically: arithmetic, exp, log, sqrt, powers, sum, products with constant matrices and vectors, the trigonometric and hyperbolic functions, all work (for $\sinh(t)/t$ and $\sin(t)/t$ use sinhc and sincu, which handle the removable singularity at the origin); type assertions, Float64(...) conversions and calls into non-generic numerical libraries do not. For determinants and inverses of matrices that depend on t, write the factorization in generic arithmetic as quadratic_forms_cgf does, or use that constructor.

Outside its domain, K may throw a DomainError (log and sqrt of a negative argument do this automatically) or return a non-finite value; both are treated as "outside the domain", and the saddlepoint iteration steps back. When the formula for K is finite outside the true domain, as with $1/(1-t)$, the domain predicate is needed. It is also cheaper than an exception.

Cumulant generating functions can be transformed: reflect (sign changes of the components), marginal, linear_combination ($Y = B'X$), sample_mean ($nK(t/n)$), shift ($X + c$), and K1 + K2 for the sum of independent vectors. A library of ready-made cumulant generating functions is described in the library.

Tail probabilities

tailprob(K, x; order=M) returns the expansions of orders $1, …, M$ of the orthant probability $P(X \ge x)$ in the vector F, with the saddlepoint tau, a status string and the full result of the underlying routine in info:

r = tailprob(K, [4.0, 1.0]; order=3)
r.F        # three approximations, of orders 1, 2 and 3
r.tau      # saddlepoint K'(τ) = x

The expansion of order $M$ keeps the terms of orders $n^{-m/2}$, $m \le 2M-1$, of the expansion in powers of $n^{-1/2}$, and has relative error $O(n^{-M})$. In practice (see the tables of the paper) each order gains one to two digits at moderate sample sizes, and the first-order approximation is accurate to a few percent.

Lower and mixed tails. lower = true gives $P(X \le x)$ and signs a mixed corner:

tailprob(K, x; lower=true)          # P(X ≤ x)
tailprob(K, x; signs=[1, -1])       # P(X₁ ≥ x₁, X₂ ≤ x₂)

The components are reflected and the probability is computed as an upper orthant probability of the reflected vector.

Saddlepoints with negative components. The expansion of Theorem 1 of the paper applies to saddlepoints in the closed positive orthant, $\tau \ge 0$, which is the case when x lies above the mean in every direction that matters. When some components of $\tau$ are negative, tailprob applies the expansion through the inclusion-exclusion identity of Section 3.3, $P(X_1 \ge x_1, X_2 \ge x_2) = P(X_2 \ge x_2) - P(X_1 < x_1, X_2 \ge x_2)$, and so on, so that every term is an orthant probability with a saddlepoint in the closed positive orthant and its own expansion. This preserves the relative error bound (Theorem 2); the paper explains why a direct evaluation would not.

Dimension one. With a univariate CGF, x may be a scalar and the expansion is that of Hauschildt and Robinson, $P(X \ge x)$ computed directly for the upper tail and $P(X \le x)$ for the lower (lower = true).

Dimensions three and more. The integrals $I(\nu)$ of the expansion are computed by Gauss quadrature in three dimensions and by randomized quasi-Monte Carlo in four or more; in the latter case info.err is a standard error. The keywords of spa_mv (nG, nGH, nqmc, nshift, qtol, ...) are passed on. The quadrature in three dimensions takes some seconds per point at orders four and five.

Very small probabilities. In two dimensions the keyword logscale = c of spa_orthant2 returns the probability scaled by $e^{-c}$, for probabilities below the range of double precision. Alternatively, compute in BigFloat (below).

Densities

density(K, x; order=M) returns the expansions of orders $1, …, M$ of the density at x (Theorem 3 of the paper; Daniels's expansion in one dimension). The expansion of order $M$ keeps the terms $n^{-m}$, $m \le M-1$, and has relative error $O(n^{-M})$. It is a finite sum of Hermite polynomials and needs no integration, in any dimension.

In three and more dimensions the terms can cancel by many orders of magnitude at high orders. The cancellation is monitored and the computation is repeated automatically in BigFloat when needed (refine = true, the default; info.refined says whether this happened).

Ratios

For a bivariate K, the joint cumulant generating function of a numerator $X_1$ and a positive denominator $X_2 > 0$:

ratio_density(K, r; order=3)              # density of X₁/X₂ at r
ratio_tailprob(K, r; order=3)             # P(X₁/X₂ ≥ r);  lower=true for P(X₁/X₂ ≤ r)

The density is the Rice expansion of $(2\pi i)^{-1}\int \exp\{K(s, -rs)\}\,\partial_2K(s,-rs)\,ds$ through the saddlepoint of $K_W(s) = K(s, -rs)$ (Theorem 4 of the paper), and the tail probability is the univariate expansion for $W = X_1 - rX_2$ at $0$. The derivatives of the amplitude $\partial_2 K$ come from the same Taylor expansion as those of $K_W$.

Several ratios. With a vector r of length $d$, K is the cumulant generating function of $(X_1, …, X_d, Y_1, …, Y_d)$, or of $(X_1, …, X_d, Y)$ for a common denominator, and ratio_density returns the joint density of $(X_1/Y_1, …, X_d/Y_d)$ at r (Theorem 4 with the amplitude expansion of Corollary 1 of the paper), while ratio_tailprob returns the orthant probability $P(R ≥ r)$, with lower and signs as in tailprob. The sample autocorrelations at lags one and two, for example, are two ratios of quadratic forms with a common denominator:

N = 50; Mc = I - ones(N, N)/N
A(j) = (diagm(j => ones(N - j)) + diagm(-j => ones(N - j)))/2
K = quadratic_forms_cgf([Mc*A(1)*Mc, Mc*A(2)*Mc, Mc])
ratio_density(K, [-1, -1]/sqrt(N); order=5).f

For the noncentral $F$ distribution, for example, with $Q_1 \sim \chi^2_m(\lambda)$ and $Q_2 \sim \chi^2_n$ independent, $F = (Q_1/m)/(Q_2/n)$:

K = CGF(t -> ncx2_cgf(m, λ)([t[1]]) + ncx2_cgf(n)([t[2]]), 2; domain = t -> t[1] < 1/2 && t[2] < 1/2)
c = f*m/n
ratio_tailprob(K, c; lower=true).F        # P(F ≤ f), orders 1..3
ratio_density(K, c).f .* (m/n)            # density of F at f

(The univariate distributions in ncx2_spa, ncf_spa and dncf_spa do this with closed-form derivatives.)

Derivatives and the saddlepoint

derivatives(K, t, N) returns all derivatives $K^{(\nu)}(t)$, $|\nu| \le N$, as a dictionary keyed by the multi-index, and saddlepoint(K, x) solves $K'(\tau) = x$ by Newton's method with backtracking. Both are what tailprob and density use internally; the derivatives at the saddlepoint are generated once, to the degree $2M + 1$ needed by the order $M$, and the whitened Taylor coefficients (see below) are generated directly rather than by substitution.

Closed-form Taylor polynomials

Automatic differentiation is the default, but a CGF can carry its Taylor polynomials in closed form through the keyword taylor, a function (t, N, M) returning the Taylor polynomial of degree N of $K(t + Mv)$ as a TaylorN (built from a coefficient table with taylor_from_coefficients), or nothing outside the domain. quadratic_forms_cgf uses this with the trace recursion of the paper evaluated directly in the whitened directions, which makes large matrices cheap, and the transformations carry the option along. Pass closedform = false to differentiate the generic formula instead.

Variable precision

Every computation runs in the floating-point type of x. Inside setprecision, with BigFloat arguments, the Taylor-mode differentiation, the saddlepoint iteration and the expansions are all done in that precision; in two and three dimensions the orthant integrals are then computed by the exact recursion of the paper instead of quadrature:

setprecision(BigFloat, 140) do            # about 40 digits
    tailprob(K, big.([4.0, 1.0]); order=5)
end

Whitening and accuracy

The terms of the multivariate expansions are evaluated for the whitened vector $Y = L^{-1}X$, $K''(\tau) = LL'$, in which they cancel far less than in the original coordinates when the components are strongly correlated under the tilted distribution (Appendix B of the paper). This needs the Taylor coefficients of $K_Y(u) = K(L^{-T}u)$, which the package obtains by differentiating the composed function directly. The substitution of the linear map into the polynomial in the original coordinates, which is what the derivative interface does when no whitened derivatives are supplied, can lose many digits at high degree; with the Taylor-mode approach the whitened coefficients are as accurate as the original ones.

status and the diagnostics in info (the cancellation indicators kappa, the quadrature checks qerr/err, the coefficient-error estimates) report the numerical quality of the evaluation. They do not measure the asymptotic error of the expansion itself, which is what the order controls.

Cost

The cost is dominated by the orthant integrals, not by the differentiation: a Taylor expansion of degree 11 of a typical cumulant generating function in three variables takes well under a millisecond, while the quadrature for a trivariate orthant probability at order five takes seconds. Densities are cheap in any dimension.

Thread safety

The functions create their own caches per call and can be called from several tasks at once. The Taylor algebras (one per dimension and degree) are created once and shared under a lock.
